# PMAY Gramin Beneficiary and Document Checklist for 2026

A PMAY-Gramin file should distinguish beneficiary identification, household and housing status, local verification, and the bank account used for assistance. Keep inspection or local-body messages with the application instead of relying only on the beneficiary reference.

Create a housing-assistance chronology from beneficiary identification through local verification, inspection, bank details, construction stage, and programme communication. Each inspection or instalment question should point to the relevant date, property or household record, and authority response. Do not use an LPG, ration-card, or general bank record as a substitute for the housing fact being checked. Preserve beneficiary-status corrections and stage evidence without overwriting the earlier record.

## Decide which housing-stage fact needs attention

A PMAY-Gramin file should begin with the household and the precise housing-stage question. The immediate issue may concern beneficiary identification, local verification, existing housing condition, bank details, inspection, construction progress, or an instalment. Identify the property or household record, beneficiary reference, and current stage before collecting documents. A ration card, LPG record, or bank account may support a particular fact, but none can stand in for the housing condition or inspection evidence being reviewed.

Compare the household, identity, housing, and account information used in the beneficiary process. If a field is wrong, determine its source owner and whether the programme requires a separate status correction. Do not create construction evidence, duplicate a household entry, or assume that appearing in a reference list guarantees every later stage or payment.

## Keep beneficiary, inspection, and bank records distinct

The household supplies accurate information and preserves evidence. Local bodies and the relevant programme route control beneficiary identification, verification, inspections, and stage status. Source authorities own identity or household documents, while the bank controls the receiving account and transaction record. A beneficiary-status error belongs with the programme or local route; a bank-name mismatch belongs with the bank; an inspection disagreement belongs with the authority that recorded the site stage.

Preserve the original beneficiary record, each inspection or local-body message, construction-stage evidence, bank details, and payment communication. If a correction occurs, keep the earlier record and acknowledgement instead of replacing it silently. For an instalment query, identify the stage, inspection, release information if available, and bank result. A bank statement can show whether money arrived, but it cannot establish that an inspection was completed or accepted.

## Follow the house from identification through each stage

Create a chronology beginning with beneficiary identification and local verification. Add the housing-status record, application or reference, inspections, stage communications, bank validation, releases, credits, and any correction. Label photographs or other progress evidence with the date and stage they genuinely show; do not use undated material as a substitute for an official inspection record.

When a stage stalls, ask the owner of that stage what fact or action is pending and preserve the response. If the household or bank details change, note when the update was recognised and which later event used it. The completed trail should explain how the beneficiary moved through the housing process and which authority answered each issue, without turning a household document, reference number, or earlier instalment into proof of future approval.

Before raising an instalment grievance, reconcile the last recorded inspection, construction stage, programme status, release information, and receiving account. Write down the first point where those records stop agreeing. This focused comparison can show whether the household needs a site inspection, beneficiary correction, release trace, or bank follow-up. It also prevents new construction evidence from being presented as though it existed at an earlier inspection.
