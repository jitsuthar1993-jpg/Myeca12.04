# ABHA Health ID and Medical Record Guide for Taxpayers

An ABHA number helps link digital health interactions, but it is not a substitute for prescriptions, bills, diagnostic reports, or insurance papers. Keep medical-expense and treatment records in their original context, and check consent and identity details before linking or sharing a health record.



## Decide what should be linked to the ABHA number

The practical decision is not simply whether to have an ABHA number. It is whether a particular health interaction or record should be linked, shared, or left in its original setting. Start with the purpose: continuing treatment, obtaining a second opinion, supporting an insurance matter, or maintaining a personal medical history. Identify the patient correctly before linking anything, then review what the receiving doctor, facility, or service actually needs. A prescription may be useful for care, while an unrelated diagnostic report or old bill may add exposure without helping the current decision.

Keep the ABHA identity separate from the contents of the medical file. The number can help connect digital interactions, but it does not replace prescriptions, discharge summaries, invoices, laboratory reports, or insurer correspondence. Preserve those records with their original dates, patient details, and issuing facilities. Before sharing, check whether the proposed record belongs to the same person and episode of care, and whether the consent presented matches the intended recipient and purpose.

## Respect the owner of each health record

The patient controls consent to sharing, but different organisations remain responsible for the records they issued or maintain. A hospital or clinic owns correction of its treatment entry, a diagnostic provider owns its report, an insurer owns its claim record, and the ABHA-facing service controls the linkage or consent transaction shown in that service. If a report contains the wrong patient detail or test result, asking only for an ABHA correction will not repair the clinical source.

For any disputed item, save the original record and identify the precise error before approaching the issuer. Ask for a corrected or clarified version through the issuer's established route, and keep its response beside the earlier copy. If the error concerns linkage rather than clinical content, record which record was linked, who received access, what consent was used, and what action was requested. Avoid circulating more medical material than the issue requires, especially when a simple identity correction can be demonstrated without the full treatment history.

## Follow consent, treatment, and expense trails separately

Maintain three chronologies instead of one mixed folder. The consent trail should show the request, recipient, purpose, date, and any withdrawal or expiry. The treatment trail should preserve clinical records in order, including corrections issued by the treating or diagnostic provider. The expense trail should connect bills, payment evidence, reimbursement submissions, and insurer responses without treating ABHA linkage as proof of payment or coverage.

When a linked record is missing or appears against the wrong person, note when it was first observed and contact the service or facility that controls that step. Record each reference and outcome without repeatedly sharing sensitive attachments. After resolution, confirm both the linkage view and the underlying source record. A clean follow-up file should let the patient explain what was shared, what was corrected, and which organisation answered each question, while keeping unrelated health information private.

Also record any decision not to link or share a record and the reason for it. A deliberate refusal, limited consent, or later withdrawal is part of the chronology, not an administrative gap. Keeping that decision beside the relevant treatment episode helps the patient answer future access questions without exposing a broader medical history.
